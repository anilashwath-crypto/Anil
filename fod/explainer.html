Inside FOD Guard

Before every cycle, one CCTV frame decides whether the guard door may lock. The camera looks at the die, compares what it sees to a master image taught once per job, and only when the part sits the right way round and nothing foreign is in the zone does the relay fire and the door bolt slide home. How the decision is made

Four stages, run in order on every cycle. Each panel on the right is drawn from the last check you ran above.

1

Teach the master, once per job

At setup you load the part correctly and capture one golden image, then drag a box around the die area. That box is the inspection zone — everything outside it (walkways, hands, the operator) is deliberately ignored, so only the die can pass or fail the check.

MASTER + INSPECTION ZONE
2

Find the part — at any small slip

The live frame is slid and twisted against the master — shifts of a few pixels, tilts to ±12°, plus the flipped positions 90/180/270° — and each pose is scored by how strongly the two images correlate. A part that merely sits a little off still scores high near 0° and passes. Best score at a wrong angle → MISORIENTED. No pose scores above the job's threshold → JOB MISSING. The dashed outline shows where the master expects the part.

LIVE FRAME vs EXPECTED POSE
3

Spot what doesn't belong

With the part located, the live frame is lined up on the master, both are normalised for lighting drift, and subtracted. What survives is only what changed. Connected patches bigger than the job's minimum size — a forgotten nut, a spanner, a chip of scrap — are flagged as foreign objects; sensor grain and glare specks fall below the size floor and are ignored.

ALIGNED DIFFERENCE MAP
4

Then — and only then — lock

The door is never locked on a hunch. Both conditions must hold on the same frame; either failure inhibits the cycle, lights the red lamp and calls the unlock URL instead. The relay is any HTTP switch (Shelly, Tasmota, ESP32) wired to the door solenoid.
